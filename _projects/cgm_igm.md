Most of a galaxy's baryons lie outside its stellar disk, in the circumgalactic medium (CGM) — a multiphase reservoir that fuels star formation, absorbs the energy of galactic winds, and records the history of feedback. Understanding this gas is central to understanding galaxies themselves: the hot CGM can regulate star formation in the disk {% cite 2023ApJ...949...21C %}, and galaxies and their atmospheres coevolve as a single system {% cite 2023ApJ...956..118P 2024ApJ...976..150V %}.

Because the CGM is intrinsically multiphase, much of our work zooms in on the small-scale physics that large simulations miss: the survival and growth of cool clouds embedded in hot winds {% cite 2022ApJ...925..199A 2024ApJ...966..181A %}, the structure of multiphase galactic outflows {% cite 2022ApJ...924...82F 2026ApJ..1003..155X %}, and the turbulent radiative mixing layers that mediate between phases {% cite 2026arXiv260604087L %}. Closer to home, we model the Milky Way's own halo gas, including the all-sky disturbance raised by the infall of the Large Magellanic Cloud {% cite 2025ApJ...983..151C %}.

Beyond galaxy halos, gas pervades the cosmic web — the Lyman-α forest and the warm-hot intergalactic medium. The low-redshift forest turns out to be a surprisingly sharp probe of feedback: efficient, long-range AGN feedback leaves a measurable imprint on its statistics {% cite 2023ApJ...945L..17T 2023AJ....166..228T %}.
