The first generation of stars formed a few hundred million years after the Big Bang out of nearly pure hydrogen and helium gas. Early simulations showed that these Population III stars were likely massive and short-lived, and that their radiation, supernovae, and metals set the initial conditions for all subsequent galaxy formation {% cite 2002Sci...295...93A %}.

Our recent work follows this story forward with the AEOS project: star-by-star cosmological simulations that track individual stars, their feedback, and the dispersal of their nucleosynthetic products through the first galaxies {% cite 2025ApJ...980...41B %}. These calculations show how unevenly the first metals are mixed into the early interstellar and intergalactic medium {% cite 2025ApJ...980...62M 2026ApJ..1002...31M %}, how the assumed Population III initial mass function shapes early chemical enrichment {% cite 2025ApJ...993....2B %}, and how to extend these results to larger volumes with calibrated semi-analytic models {% cite 2025arXiv251011629H %}.

This field is now becoming observational: lensed systems at high redshift are reaching the sensitivity needed to test these predictions directly {% cite 2025ApJ...993L..17V %}, and the chemical fingerprints of the first stars may also be recoverable from damped Lyman-α absorbers near the epoch of reionization {% cite 2026JCAP...02..077V %}.
